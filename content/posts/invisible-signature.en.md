# The Invisible Signature

Anthropic now embeds an invisible watermark in text generated by Claude. The company signed the EU AI Act's **Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-Generated Content** under Article 50(2), and published what that means in practice: models launched in the EU on or after August 2, 2026 mark their output in machine-readable ways. Text carries an embedded watermark; supported files carry C2PA **Content Credentials**.

It is an attempt to answer "was this made by AI?" with a technical signal. But the gap between what the watermark says and what people will assume it says is where the real story is.

## A mark woven into the text

Claude's watermark is imperceptible and does not change meaning, quality, or readability. Because it is part of the text, it travels when the text is copied and pasted, and it may survive some editing. Marking happens at the model level, so it appears across surfaces: the Claude Platform API, Claude, Claude Code, Claude Cowork, and Claude Tag.

Files work differently. When Claude generates a supported file such as a PNG or JPEG, it attaches signed provenance metadata following C2PA, an open standard used across the industry.

Detection is still in **private preview**. Access is limited to eligible organizations consistent with the EU Code, such as regulators, law enforcement, media, fact-checkers, independent researchers, educational organizations, and EU civil society groups. Enterprises that need to check marks on their own Claude output can also get access.

## The limits are the interesting part

Anthropic's own documentation is unusually candid. A detected Claude mark is a signal that content **may have been processed by Claude**, not a conclusive proof of provenance. For example:

- Claude may not be the original author. People use it to proofread, translate, summarize, or convert files. Output can carry a Claude mark even when the underlying ideas, text, or data came from somewhere else.
- The content may have changed after Claude processed it. Marked text can be modified, excerpted, or combined with other material.

The reverse is also true: **the absence of a mark does not mean the content was not AI-generated.** Text may have been heavily edited, paraphrased, translated, or mixed into other writing. It may be too short for a reliable signal. A file's metadata may have been stripped by conversion, re-saving, or a screenshot. The model may not support that marking type. On Sonnet 4.6, non-default `temperature`, `top_p`, or `top_k` values can make the text watermark unreliable.

A watermark is therefore not proof of authorship. It is proof of **processing**. It says "this text passed through Claude." It does not say who wrote it, whose idea it was, or who is accountable.

## Absence is not evidence

Security and law both have a recurring failure mode: treating the absence of a signal as proof of the opposite. With watermarks, that failure is especially dangerous. A well-intentioned writer who uses a model to polish a draft may look "AI-generated." A bad actor can edit the text enough to remove the mark. The result can be a verification surface that **punishes the honest and leaves the deceptive untouched**.

The better mental model is not a lie detector but a **chain of custody**. A record of where a document came from, who edited it, which tools it passed through, and who approved the final version. A single mark is one link in that chain, not the chain itself.

## Who gets to check?

Perhaps the most important question is not technical: who can access the detection API? Today, access is limited to regulators and certain institutions. That creates an inevitable asymmetry. A newsroom or a citizen cannot directly check whether the text in front of them carries a mark, while a large institution or a government can.

Transparency only means something when **everyone can verify**, not just when marking exists. Otherwise the watermark becomes less a public verification tool than an institutional compliance tool.

## Questions we have not answered

- If a text says "passed through Claude," what does that give the reader? Without knowing the author, what does provenance even mean?
- Should a text that carries a watermark but whose ideas are entirely human count as "AI-generated"? Who decides?
- If removing a watermark becomes easy, where does responsibility land: the user, the tool provider, or the distribution platform?
- If the detection API is only open to institutions, does "transparency" become one-way surveillance?
- If every text can be marked one day, will unmarked text become automatically suspect? Or will unmarked text become the norm?

A watermark can say "this text passed through AI." It cannot say "you can trust this text." Confusing the two turns a technical signal into a moral verdict.
